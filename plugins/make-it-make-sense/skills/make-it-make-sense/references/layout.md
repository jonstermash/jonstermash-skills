# Layout and medium reference

Loaded by **Pass 2 · Structure**. How the piece looks and reads at a glance — before anyone reads a word.

## Match the medium

The shape of the fix depends on where it lands:

- **Slides** — a headline plus a few short phrases, one idea per slide, the detail in the speaker notes.
- **Docs** — short prose, bottom-line first, headings that carry the outline.
- **Email** — the ask in the first line.
- **Diagrams** — see "Diagrams read from the subject" below.

## Shape it for scanning

A simplified piece should be **navigable at a glance** — the reader's eye finds the structure before they read a word.

- **Break lists out.** When naming several things, give them their own lines as bullets instead of burying them in a sentence. Three bullets register instantly; the same three items inside a sentence have to be untangled first.
- **Build a hierarchy.** Use headings, subheads, and indentation so the shape shows through structure alone. Someone reading only the headings should still come away knowing roughly what's there.
- **Emphasize with intent.** Reach for **bold**, _italic_, or underline on the few words that carry the most weight — a key number, the decisive term, the actual ask. Keep it to a handful: if half the page is bold, nothing stands out. Spotlight only what's on-message, never a detail that pulls the eye off the point.

## Whitespace and reading direction do real work

Layout is not decoration; it steers the eye, and a reader parses the arrangement before the words.

- **Give it room to breathe.** Whitespace around a block is what makes it read as one unit. A wall with no gaps forces the reader to find the seams themselves; crowding a headline against its body erases the beat between claim and support. Space is how the page signals "this goes together, that's separate."
- **Respect the reading path.** For left-to-right, top-to-bottom readers, the eye lands top-left and travels down the left edge — so the most important thing goes top and left, and a heading sits *above* and *left of* what it heads, never adrift to the side. A right-aligned label or a caption floating left of its subject fights the path and costs a beat.
- **Align to a grid.** Ragged starts read as noise; a consistent left edge and consistent spacing let the reader stop noticing the container and read the content. Alignment is invisible when it's right and distracting when it's not.

## Diagrams read from the subject

Diagrams are content before they're visuals — the same clarity pass applies to what a diagram *says*. Put the subject/actor (usually the user) as the visual focus and the origin of the flow, and make every arrow mean something.

- Choose a reading direction that matches the mental model (top-down from the actor is usually safest).
- Every arrow encodes a true relationship and direction; don't add arrows for decoration or drop them and lean on floating boxes.
- Represent supporting elements as what they are (a key that "unlocks," not a co-equal box).

The failure mode is a diagram that's technically complete but reads wrong: a misleading left-to-right chain, missing arrows, a helper element drawn as a peer, the subject buried instead of centered.
